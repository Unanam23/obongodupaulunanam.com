Issue 024 The Accountability Gap

The Office That Paid N33.75 Billion to Nigeria's Poorest Households Would Not Show the Auditors the Ledger. So the Case Went to a Court Nigeria Has Ignored 44 Times.

In 2023 the National Cash Transfer Office says it paid N33.75 billion to 3.3 million of the poorest households in the country. When the Auditor General asked for the payment ledger that would prove it, the office's accounts staff obstructed and denied access, in the auditors' words, and the report that says so has been with the National Assembly since July. Nobody has been summoned. Last week SERAP filed the whole N78.8 billion in cash transfer queries at the ECOWAS Court as a human rights case. It is the right court on the law and the wrong one for results. By ActionAid's count, Nigeria has left 44 of that court's judgments unenforced.

By Obongodu Paul Unanam. Published Thursday 29 October 2026. Nine minutes. On public money.

The Auditor General's report on the federal accounts for 2023 has been with the National Assembly since July 17. Most of it is the usual inventory: vouchers paid without a prepayment audit, store items that never arrived, diesel advances to staff. One query is not usual. The National Cash Transfer Office says that in 2023 it paid N33.751 billion to 3,295,207 households in 35 states, the poorest people whose names the Nigerian state has ever written down. When the auditors asked for the Remita statement, the digital ledger that would show each payment leaving a bank and landing against a name on the National Social Register, the office did not produce it. In the auditors' words, access to the records was obstructed and denied by the office's accounts staff. Their recommendation is that the National Programme Manager, Abdullahi Imam, be summoned before the Public Accounts Committees to show where the money went, and that if he cannot, the whole N33.75 billion be recovered and returned to the treasury.

That was fifteen weeks ago. Nobody has been summoned. Last week SERAP took the question to a court in Abuja that Nigeria has learned it can ignore.

Some context first, because 2023 is not a random year for this office. It is the year the ministry above it came apart. On January 8, 2024, President Tinubu suspended the Minister of Humanitarian Affairs, Betta Edu, over a N585 million payment routed into a private account. The EFCC had already arrested Halima Shehu, who ran the National Social Investment Programme Agency, over N37.1 billion said to have moved through the ministry under the previous minister, Sadiya Umar Farouq, who was invited for questioning in the same weeks. The President suspended the social investment programmes outright. In April 2024 the commission announced it had recovered N32.7 billion and $445,000 from the ministry. None of that is the N33.75 billion in this audit. It is the weather the money was moving through.

The audit does not say the money was stolen. It says nobody can show that it was not, and that the people who could show it declined to. The office's reply, when the finding reached the papers this week, was that it shared electronic data trails with the auditors by email and that the funds moved within verified payment structures. I do not know which account is true. That is the point. An audit query is supposed to be the beginning of a process that decides between two accounts, and in Nigeria it is nearly always the end of one.

Do the arithmetic the office invites. N33.751 billion across 3,295,207 households is about N10,200 a household for the year. The programme's stipend at the time was N5,000 a month. If every naira went where the office says, the average poor household in the scheme received two months of support in twelve. If some of it did not, the people it was taken from are the ones least able to tell anybody.

Now the route the Constitution provides. The Auditor General reports to the National Assembly, and the Public Accounts Committees of each chamber are the bodies that summon officials and ask them to explain. I have watched that route this month. The six FCT area council chairmen, facing about N100 billion in queries, asked the House committee for September 22 as their date, did not come, did not send anyone, and then let a seven day final summons pass. NNPCL and INEC did not turn up to answer for N802 billion. The committee's response, each time, was to say the word final again. Nothing in the Constitution or in either chamber's rules sets a date by which an audit query must be answered, and nothing says what follows if it is not. The cash transfer report will go into the same room as those, and it will wait its turn behind them.

SERAP knows this, which is why it wrote to the President on September 5, gave him seven days, and then filed suit ECW/CCJ/APP/42/26 at the ECOWAS Court of Justice. The case does something I find clever and a little sad at the same time. It takes an audit query and reframes it as a human rights violation: the money was meant for the rights to social security and an adequate standard of living of 3.2 million households, the government cannot show it was delivered, and failing to account for it breaches Nigeria's obligations to those people. The reliefs ask the court to declare the breach, order a full account, order an independent investigation, order recovery, and order that the households who were listed and not paid be paid. On the law I think SERAP is right. The court has taken this kind of case from this applicant before, on education in 2010 and on the Niger Delta in 2012, and it is the one court in the region where an ordinary organisation can put the Nigerian state in the dock without the state's permission.

It is also the one court whose judgments the Nigerian state has made a habit of not obeying. Richard Akinnola told a SERAP audience in May that the federal government has refused to enforce at least 44 ECOWAS Court judgments, a count ActionAid Nigeria confirmed in March last year. The court ordered N30 million for Agba Jalingo in 2021 over 34 days of detention and torture. It ordered N2 million each for three EndSARS protesters in July 2024. Neither, he said, has been paid. I wrote in August about the court's 2020 order that Nigeria repeal a section of its Cybercrime Act, which took four years to act on while journalists kept being arrested under it. The court has no bailiff. Its protocol leaves enforcement to an authority each member state is supposed to designate, and the honest summary of Nigerian practice is that a judgment the government dislikes is received, filed and forgotten.

The domestic process has no clock. The regional court has a clock and no hands. SERAP chose the second because the first has never once run out.

So picture the sequence from the office's side. The auditors asked for the ledger and were refused. The National Assembly will ask for the programme manager and may or may not get him, on no particular date. A court in Abuja will, in a year or two, order an account of the money, and the government can decline to give one at no cost it has ever been made to pay. At every stage the office holds the only document that would settle the question, and at no stage is it required to open it. The money for the poorest Nigerians is the money with the least paper behind it, and the one office that holds the paper is the one nobody can compel.

There is a detail that makes this more than a story about 2023. The office that could not produce the 2023 ledger is the office now running the Household Prosperity and Empowerment Cash Transfer, the successor programme launched in December 2023 on an $800 million World Bank credit, which pays N75,000 to each household in three instalments and which the minister said in March had reached 9,178,837 beneficiaries in its first tranche. The National Programme Manager who presented those figures is Abdullahi Imam, the same official the auditors want summoned about the old money. The new programme's numbers rest on the credibility of an office that, on the auditors' account, would not open its books for the last one. The National Identity Management Commission says it has verified 11.8 million of the new beneficiary records. That is reassuring until you remember that the 2023 payments also went to people on a register, and the question was never whether the names existed. It was whether the money reached them.

The 2023 payments also went to names on a register. The question was never whether the names existed. It was whether the money reached them, and the office that knows will not say.

SERAP's letter in September threatened one other route, and I think it is the stronger one. The World Bank's Inspection Panel can take a complaint from people harmed by a Bank financed project, and the Bank, unlike the ECOWAS Court, holds something the government wants: the next $800 million. A lender that stops disbursing until a ledger is produced will get a ledger. A court whose last 44 orders were ignored will get a 45th. If I were advising SERAP I would have led with the Panel and kept the court for the declaration, because the declaration is what the court is good at and the money is what the Bank is good at. SERAP may yet file both. I hope it does.

What would close the gap is the same thing it always is. A date. A rule that an audit query unanswered within a fixed time stops the next release of funds to that office, so that the people who hold the ledger have a reason to open it that does not depend on a committee finding the will to say final and mean it. Nigeria does not have that rule, and the National Assembly, which would have to write it, is the body whose summons are currently being ignored.

The Remita statement exists. Somewhere on a bank's servers and the office's own, every one of those 3,295,207 payments has a timestamp and a destination. The auditors asked to see it and were refused. A regional court will now be asked to order what a committee of the National Assembly could have asked for in July. The office's answer will tell us more than its reply to the auditors did. Or it will not answer, which is also an answer, and one Nigeria has given that court 44 times.
