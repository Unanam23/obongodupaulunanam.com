Issue 022 Thursday 15 October 2026

The EFCC Has Had Nineteen Years to Charge Atiku. This Week, 128 Days Before the Election, It Reopened His File Instead.

The EFCC wrote its report on Atiku Abubakar in 2006, while he held immunity. The immunity ended in May 2007. No charge followed, and no decision not to charge either. On Monday a former lawmaker spent six hours at the commission's headquarters adopting a petition about that report, and by Wednesday the commission said an investigation had commenced. The problem is not that the file reopened. It is that nothing ever required it to close.

Published elsewhere

  • TheCable, May 2026

    Government Built a Whistleblowing Portal But Forgot to Enact Laws to Make Whistleblowing Safe

    Nigeria's federal whistleblowing policy has no statutory force. It cannot be enforced in any Nigerian court. This piece follows a former civil servant who used the government's own reporting channel and spent the next year losing his job, facing criminal charges, and having police sent to his home.

  • Global Policy Journal, University of Durham, June 2026

    When the Extraterritorial Enforcer Steps Back: West Africa

    What happens to anti-corruption enforcement in West Africa when the external pressure that historically drove compliance withdraws, and which regional institutions actually step into that space.

  • International Law Blog, June 2026

    Legislative Omission as Treaty Breach: Nigeria, the AUCPCC, and What the ECOWAS Court Has Left Undone

    Nigeria ratified a convention requiring statutory whistleblower protection in 2006. Three separate bills have died in committee since 2001. This piece argues that two decades of legislative inaction has crossed into a breach of binding treaty obligation.

  • IntLawGrrls, June 2026

    UNCAC Article 33 and the Absent Obligation

    UNCAC Article 33 only asks states to consider whistleblower protection, never to enact it. This piece argues the gap left by that soft language is not gender neutral.

  • BAC Afrocentric Story Contest, first place, 2026

    The Weight of a Gown

    A story about what it costs to become who you were told you would be, and what you carry long after the ceremony is over.

  • Dear Aliens Writing Contest, third place, 2026

    What We Do When the Light Goes Out

    An international competition asked what single document you would send to arriving extraterrestrials. This piece placed third from thousands of global entries, and is about generators, Nigeria, and the talent for filling the space between what you have and what you need.

  • Corporate Compliance Insights, July 2026

    Your Whistleblower Hotline Does Not Work the Same Way in Nigeria

    Most multinationals deploy internal reporting infrastructure as a uniform global system. This piece argues that assumption breaks down in Nigeria, where no statutory whistleblower protection exists.

  • Africa Is a Country, August 2026

    The Prosecutions That Never Happen

    Nigeria's EFCC announces thousands of convictions a year, but Human Rights Watch has found only four against nationally prominent political figures across the agency's entire history. This piece traces that gap back to the EFCC's founding statute, which hands the president appointment and removal power over the very agency meant to hold his office accountable.

  • SCC Times, SCC Online Blog, July 2026

    When Anti-Corruption Agencies Answer to the Executive: Nigeria, India, and the Independence Problem

    A comparative look at why Nigeria's EFCC and India's CBI, two of the most visible anti-corruption agencies in the Global South, both answer to the executive that appoints their leadership. Written for India's leading legal research platform, the piece argues that enforcement activity and institutional independence are not the same thing, and that neither country has built the second one yet.

  • Journal du Droit Transnational, August 2026

    Deference Deferred: The ECOWAS Court, Nigeria's Cybercrime Act, and the Enforcement Problem in Regional Cyber Law Harmonisation

    In 2020, the ECOWAS Community Court ordered Nigeria to repeal a provision of its Cybercrime Act for violating free expression rights. Nigeria took four years to act, during which the provision kept being used to arrest journalists and critics. This piece uses that delay to examine why regional courts in West Africa have no real mechanism to make a member state comply on any particular timeline.

  • D+C Development and Cooperation, September 2026

    Protecting the People Who Know Where the Money Went

    Anti-corruption agencies depend on insiders coming forward, but Nigeria still has no law protecting whistleblowers from retaliation once they do. The piece argues that development programmes measuring anti-corruption progress by institutional capacity alone are missing the part that actually determines whether those institutions get any information at all.

  • Journal of Arts and Sociological Research, September 2026

    Regulating the Pre Campaign Finance Gap: Nomination Fees, Party Primaries, and Electoral Transparency in Nigeria

    Nigeria's 2026 Electoral Act tightened campaign finance rules but left the financing of party primaries, nomination fees, and consensus arrangements almost untouched, even though those processes decide who appears on the ballot at all. Co authored with Godwin Agaba Ochube and Umar Yakubu of the University of Uyo, this peer reviewed article maps that disclosure gap against the 2026/2027 primary cycle and argues that closing it needs both INEC regulatory interpretation and sustained civil society litigation strategy. Vol. 13(6), open access, Crossref DOI.

About

I graduated from the University of Uyo Faculty of Law in 2025. My dissertation examined Nigeria's anti corruption legal framework, and it left me with a question I have not stopped asking since: why does enforcement fail so consistently when the legislation says otherwise.

Most of my research sits where anti corruption institutional design, UNCAC implementation and whistleblower protection meet. I am particularly interested in how the African human rights system treats people who report wrongdoing. The short answer is: not well. The longer answer is what I keep publishing about.

I also write fiction and personal essays. The best governance writing and the best storytelling do the same thing. They make you feel the weight of something you could previously walk past without noticing.

I am not yet called to the bar, so what I publish is legal and policy analysis rather than legal practice, and it is framed that way. During my studies I interned at the Akwa Ibom State Ministry of Justice, at Nwoko and Co, Access Law House and Humanity Voice Foundation. I am now an intern on Blocklaw Consulting's blockchain and digital assets law programme.

What I am working on

Anti corruption institutional design. The gap between the EFCC and ICPC's structure on paper and their function in practice, and what a redesigned architecture would need to close it.

Whistleblower protection. Why the AUCPCC obligation to legislate protection has gone unmet for nearly twenty years, and what the African human rights system could do about it.

Asset recovery. Where POCA, UNCAC and the international cooperation frameworks quietly stop working, and what happens to funds after recovery is announced.

Governance transparency. How information moves, or fails to move, between institutions and the people those institutions exist to serve.
