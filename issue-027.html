Issue 027 The Accountability Gap

Nigeria Told Africa This Week How Recovered Loot Should Be Accounted For. Its Own Senate Passed a Bill to Do It at Home in July and Rescinded It Seven Days Later.

Abuja is hosting the first general assembly of Africa's asset recovery practitioners, and the Attorney General has told the continent to judge recovery by what citizens receive and to report independently on how recovered assets are used. At home those assets sit with 18 directorates, each keeping its own books, and nothing I can find in the law obliges any of them to show the public. On July 9 the Senate passed a bill for one database, quarterly disclosure and independent audits. On July 16 it took the vote back. The fullest public account of how returned loot was spent that I know of was paid for by Britain and Switzerland.

By Obongodu Paul Unanam. Published Thursday 19 November 2026. Nine minutes. On asset recovery.

On Saturday, at a briefing in Abuja ahead of this week's gathering of African asset recovery officials, the ICPC's Hassan Mohamed was asked whether any country on the continent was ahead of Nigeria. He said it was hard to tell. Some countries recover assets without ever saying what they recovered, he explained, so you cannot be sure they are recovering anything at all. Asked for Nigeria's own figures, he said he did not have the information at the briefing.

I am not going to make much of an official without a spreadsheet at a press conference. He also made a promise, and the promise matters more. The ICPC, he said, will publish what it has recovered, with the status of each asset and who owned it. "Nothing is hidden. It is a public document." I think he means it. I also think he was describing a choice the commission has made, because as far as I can read the law, nothing obliges it to publish. The bill that would have written a duty of disclosure into law passed the Senate on July 9 and was rescinded on July 16.

The gathering is the first general assembly of the African Asset Recovery Practitioners' Forum. It opened on Tuesday and closes tomorrow. The forum was launched in Nairobi in April 2025 to put into practice the African Union's Common African Position on Asset Recovery, adopted in February 2020, and one of that position's pillars is the management of assets after they are recovered. Musa Adamu Aliyu, the ICPC chairman, chaired its interim steering committee when it was set up.

The Attorney General, Lateef Fagbemi, opened the assembly with a speech I agree with almost line by line. Asset recovery, he said, is about accountability and public trust, and tracing an account or winning a forfeiture order is only where it begins. He wants it judged by how openly recovered assets are managed and by what citizens actually receive, more than by the size of the sums announced. He asked the forum for a common continental procedure running from tracing all the way to disposal, and for independent reporting on how recovered assets are used. History, he said, would not judge the assembly by "the beauty of its declarations".

So hold Nigeria to his test.

Start with the law. The Proceeds of Crime (Recovery and Management) Act, which President Buhari signed in May 2022, does not give the management of recovered assets to one body. It gives it to eighteen. Each relevant organisation it names, among them the EFCC, the ICPC, the police, Customs, the NDLEA and the DSS, must set up its own Proceeds of Crime Management Directorate. Section 3 tells each directorate to keep "a central database" of the assets its own organisation has seized and recovered, with statistics and an inventory recording where each asset is, what it is worth and what condition it is in. Eighteen central databases. Each organisation also runs its own Confiscated and Forfeited Properties Account at the Central Bank, and its head reports on that account to the Minister of Finance.

What the Act does not do, on my reading, is require any of those databases to be published, sent to the National Assembly or gathered in one place. Section 71 says the accounts are to be audited according to guidelines from the Auditor General. Whether those audits happen, and what they find, the Act does not require anyone to tell you.

Senator Idiat Adebule's amendment was an attempt to change that design. She brought it to the floor in 2025, arguing that eighteen directorates meant duplicated work and weak safeguards for the assets themselves. As the ICIR summarised the bill, it would have created one agency to hold, manage and dispose of recovered assets, folded the directorates into it within a year and left investigation and prosecution where they are now. It provided for a central asset database, quarterly disclosure of disposals, independent audits and an oversight panel. It went through first and second reading, committee, a public hearing and consideration of all 51 clauses one at a time. The Senate passed it on Thursday July 9, and the Senate President, Godswill Akpabio, commended his colleagues on it.

On Thursday July 16 the Senate Leader, Opeyemi Bamidele, moved to rescind the passage. He cited problems of drafting, law and policy found after the vote, and the need for the bill to conform with "international best practices" on asset recovery. When a senator raised an observation, the Senate President ruled it out of order, because the chamber was considering the rescission and not the substance of the bill. The motion carried unanimously on a voice vote. In August a coalition of civil society groups asked the Senate to explain what new concern had appeared after a complete committee process, and pointed out that Bamidele had sat on the committee that recommended the agency.

Five days after the rescission, on July 21, the House of Representatives adopted a motion lamenting that Nigeria has no centralised, publicly accessible register of anything it has recovered since 1999. One chamber had just taken back its vote for a central database. The other was asking why the country did not have one.

I do not know who wanted the bill gone. The ICIR, quoting a source it did not name, reported in September that the Senate President led the reversal after the EFCC and others persuaded him, and said the Senate, the EFCC and his aides did not respond to its questions. I cannot confirm that, and I am not going to write as though I can. The EFCC's case against the bill is better documented. According to the ICIR, the commission told the National Assembly that a new agency would duplicate existing institutions, add to government spending, provoke conflicts over jurisdiction and expose recovered assets to corruption and political interference. One of its documents cited the experience of AMCON and warned of "turf wars".

Some of that deserves a fair hearing. One agency in charge of every recovered asset in the country would need watching too. But the objection would carry more weight if the present arrangement were cleaner. In July the EFCC put forfeited vehicles up for sale on an online platform it built and runs itself, and the Nigeria Association of Auctioneers said the commission had no legal basis to be both custodian and seller of the same assets.

Read the objections again, too. Every one of them is about the agency. I have not seen one aimed at the database or the audits, and the ICIR noted that the bill already met some of the commission's concerns with certified custody records and independent audits. The disclosure clauses could have been separated from the agency and kept. Nobody tried. They went out with everything else, and four months on I can find no sign of the bill back on the floor.

Every objection I have seen was aimed at the new agency. Not one touched the database or the audits, and the Senate took them back with it.

What makes this hard to excuse is that Nigeria knows how to account for recovered money. It has done it. In December 2017 Switzerland returned $322.5 million in stolen funds linked to General Sani Abacha's family. The return came with a memorandum between Nigeria, Switzerland and the World Bank that set out where the money would go and how it would be monitored. It went to the poorest households through the National Cash Transfer Office, alongside money from a World Bank loan, and civil society monitors led by the Africa Network for Environment and Economic Justice followed it from the Central Bank through the payment agents to the homes. In one round they sent out more than 700 monitors. In Akamkpa, in Cross River, they watched payments reach about 316 people. Their final account puts the total paid out between September 2018 and April 2022 at $335.9 million, the principal plus $13.4 million in interest, or about N123.7 billion.

The watching was paid for from abroad. Britain funded the monitoring from April 2018 until January 2021, and Switzerland paid for the final stretch.

Nigeria has followed recovered money all the way to a household in Akamkpa. It did so with Switzerland and the World Bank at the table and Britain paying the monitors.

Here is the detail I have not been able to put down since I wrote about cash transfers in October. The office that paid out the Abacha money with monitors behind it was the National Cash Transfer Office. When the Auditor General examined its 2023 transactions, he found that the same office could not identify the beneficiaries of N33.75 billion it had paid out, and that its management did not respond to the query. I doubt the office forgot how to keep records in a year. What I think changed is that nobody outside it was checking.

That is the gap I want to name this week. Nigeria accounts properly for recovered money when someone else insists on it and someone else pays for it. For the money its own agencies recover through its own courts, the law keeps eighteen sets of books and obliges nobody to show them to the public. The latest attempt to change that lasted seven days. A country in that position can still host a continental assembly on asset recovery, and it is right that it does. It should expect its guests to notice.

None of the fix needs the agency the Senate sent back for review. Section 3 already makes every directorate keep a database, statistics and an inventory. A short amendment could require each of them to publish those records every quarter, in a common format and in one place, showing each asset, its value, where it is held, its status in court and what finally became of it, and could require the section 71 audits to be published every year. The Ministry of Justice already coordinates the Act's implementation across the eighteen bodies, so the consolidated list has an obvious home. The ICPC says its records are a public document. Then they need a date, and a format the other seventeen can copy.

Nairobi ended with a declaration, and Abuja probably will too. Fagbemi has already told us how to read it. On Saturday Mohamed said that when a country recovers assets and tells nobody what it recovered, you cannot be sure it is recovering anything at all. He was talking about other countries. It is a fair test. I would like Nigeria to pass it with its own numbers rather than with a speech, and that will not happen by tomorrow. I would settle for a date.
