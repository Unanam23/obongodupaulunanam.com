Issue 025 The Accountability Gap

Nigeria's Own Courts, Auditors and Committees Have Found $123 Billion It Is Owed. A Private Lawyer Has Had to Give the Senate Fourteen Days to Go and Ask for It.

Femi Falana's letter to the Senate President says the federation is owed $123.17 billion and N66.4 billion, and every line of the figure was found by the state itself: a Supreme Court consent judgment from 2018, Federal High Court judgments for Akwa Ibom, Rivers and Bayelsa, a House committee report from 2016, NEITI's statutory report, the cabinet's own refinery approvals. None of those findings put a duty on anyone to collect. The Senate he wrote to adopted a $200 billion oil theft report a year ago this week, has not released its N210 trillion NNPCL findings, and overturned its own committee's arrest warrant within two days. I do not think a court will order it to act. I also think the letter is the only deadline this money has had in eight years.

By Obongodu Paul Unanam. Published Thursday 5 November 2026. Nine minutes. On public money.

On October 28 Femi Falana wrote to the President of the Senate and gave him fourteen days. The letter, which ThisDay published on Monday, says the federation is owed $123.17 billion and N66.4 billion in royalties, taxes, dividends and the proceeds of stolen crude, and asks the Senate to begin legal steps to recover it. If it does not, Falana says, he will go to the Federal High Court for an order compelling it to. I have read the letter twice and I do not think a court will give him that order. I also think it is the only deadline anybody has set on this money in eight years, and that is the subject of this issue.

Start with where the number comes from, because it is not Falana's. Every line of it was found by the Nigerian state.

The largest, $62 billion, comes out of a judgment of the Supreme Court. In October 2018 Akwa Ibom, where I am writing this, together with Rivers and Bayelsa, won a consent judgment against the federal government over the deep offshore production sharing contracts of 1993. The contracts were priced for oil at $9 a barrel and carried a clause, Section 16 of the Act, requiring a review once oil passed $20. Oil passed $20 in 2003. Nobody reviewed anything. The court ordered the government to recover what the failure had cost, a committee was set up to count it, and the committee came back with $62 billion owed by the six international oil companies in joint ventures with NNPC. The Attorney General demanded payment. The Minister of State for Petroleum then called the sum a lost opportunity and said the government might recover something, but not $62 billion. Falana wrote to the Attorney General about this in 2021, citing Section 287 of the Constitution, which obliges every authority in the country to enforce a decision of the Supreme Court. Judgments of the Federal High Court in favour of Akwa Ibom and Rivers, worth about $3.3 billion with interest, and of Bayelsa, for $951 million in June 2021, have followed. The letter says none has been obeyed.

The next $29 billion is stolen crude. A NIMASA legal team found about 60 million barrels, worth $12.7 billion, discharged at the port of Philadelphia between 2011 and 2014 without record. A House of Representatives ad hoc committee in 2016 confirmed a further $17 billion in crude and gas landed at Houston and Lake Charles the same way. That committee was chaired by Johnson Agbonayinma, who readers of this newsletter met three weeks ago as the petitioner who got the EFCC to reopen its file on Atiku. The $23.1 billion is NLNG dividends: NNPCL holds the government's 49 percent of the gas company, NLNG has paid more than $47 billion in dividends over 26 years, and Falana says the government's share never reached the Federation Account. Governor Makinde made a version of the same allegation last month and the presidency called it false. The $6.07 billion and the N66.4 billion are NEITI's. Its 2022 and 2023 industry report, released in September 2024 and sent to both chambers of the National Assembly in November 2024 as the NEITI Act requires, listed those sums as royalties, gas flare penalties and taxes the companies owed and had not paid as of mid 2024. The last $2.98 billion is the Port Harcourt, Warri and Kaduna refinery rehabilitation contracts the Federal Executive Council approved in 2021. Warri stopped working on January 25 this year. Port Harcourt shut for maintenance on May 29.

I would not stake this newsletter on the arithmetic. Some of the lines overlap. The $62 billion is contested by the companies and, in effect, by the government that won it. The NLNG figure is a claim NNPCL disputes. But I do not need the total to be exact to make the point, because the point is not the sum. It is that a court, a committee of the Attorney General's office, a House committee, a statutory agency and the cabinet itself each produced a finding that money is owed, and that not one of those findings created a duty in any named person to go and collect it.

Which is why the choice of addressee is strange, and revealing. The Senate cannot recover a dollar. Royalties are collected by the upstream regulator, taxes by the FIRS, proceeds of crime by the EFCC, and judgments by the Attorney General as the government's chief law officer, since Section 287 binds every authority in the country to enforce them. What the Senate can do is investigate and resolve, and Section 88 of the Constitution says it may do so, not that it must. Falana is asking a court to order a legislature to exercise a discretion. Courts do not do that, and he has argued enough separation of powers cases to know it. So why write to Akpabio at all?

Because the Senate has already said most of this itself. A year ago this week it adopted the interim report of its ad hoc committee on crude oil theft, which found more than $200 billion in crude proceeds unaccounted for between 2015 and 2023, including an $81 billion gap between what NNPC and the Central Bank each said the crude had earned in 2016 and 2017, and asked for a mandate to track and recover the money at home and abroad. Its public accounts committee spent this year on the Auditor General's finding of more than N210 trillion in unresolved entries in NNPCL's accounts from 2017 to 2023. In March Falana asked the Senate for that committee's report under the Freedom of Information Act. The Senate acknowledged the letter and did nothing. In June he sued it, suit FHC/ABJ/CS/783/2026. No hearing date has been fixed. When the same committee issued a warrant for the arrest of the former NNPCL chief executive for ignoring its invitations, the full Senate overturned the warrant within two days and rebuked the committee for risking the National Assembly's credibility. The letter of October 28 is addressed to the body that has found the most and done the least with it, and I think that is the point of addressing it.

What Nigeria lacks is not findings. A court found the money, an auditor found it, a committee found it. What it lacks is one official whose job it is to go and collect, and a date by which he must say whether he has.

Set the letter beside the EFCC's own record on these sums. When NEITI released the report that put the oil companies' debts at $6 billion, the chairman of the commission stood at the launch and said the EFCC had recovered more than N1 billion on the strength of past NEITI reports and would look into the new one. One billion naira. Against the figures in the report he was holding, that is a rounding error, and he offered it as a credential. I do not doubt the commission would like the money. The oil companies that owe it are the ones with the lawyers, the lobbyists and the production the government cannot afford to interrupt, and nothing in the NEITI Act or the EFCC Act says what must happen, or by when, once a sum is published as owed. The report goes to the National Assembly, the National Assembly notes it, and the next report two years later lists the same debts plus interest.

There is a reason this matters more this week than most. I wrote in July that Nigeria owes N159 trillion and that nobody is required to explain why. Falana's letter makes the connection that the debt debate never makes: a government that collected what its own courts and auditors say it is owed would not be borrowing. He is right about that even if his total is high by half. The deep offshore money alone would cover years of external borrowing. Instead the government that consented to the judgment in 2018 amended the Act in 2019, called the arrears a lost opportunity, and went back to the Eurobond market.

The Senate adopted a report saying $200 billion was unaccounted for, asked for a mandate to recover it, and then spent a year refusing to release its next report. A lawyer now has to sue it to read its own findings.

So here is what I expect. The fourteen days run out around November 11. Falana will file, as he filed in June. The Senate will acknowledge receipt, as it did in March. A judge will eventually hold, correctly, that the court cannot direct the legislature to legislate or investigate, and the money will stay wherever it went. None of that makes the letter pointless. It is the only document in eight years that attaches a date to the deep offshore judgment, and dates are what this newsletter keeps finding missing. An audit query with no deadline is an opinion. A judgment nobody must enforce is a press release. A Senate report that recommends a recovery mandate and never asks for one is a year of hearings.

What would change it is not a new agency. It is a duty. The NEITI Act already sends every report to the National Assembly. Add one section: within ninety days of each report, the Attorney General states in writing, for every sum listed as owed, what has been recovered, what is in litigation and what has been written off, and why. Do the same for every judgment in the federation's favour. Publish the ledger. The money would not all come back. But the official who declined to collect it would, for the first time, have to put his name to the decision, and Falana would not have to write letters to the one institution that cannot cash a cheque.

The letter asks the Senate to request the 2016 committee report from the Clerk of the House. That is Agbonayinma's report, the one on $17 billion of crude landed in Texas and Louisiana without paperwork, and it is now ten years old. Nobody in government has said where it is. I would like to be told it is in a file. I suspect it is in the same place as the money.
