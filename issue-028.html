Issue 028 The Accountability Gap

Nigeria's Constitution Says Any Citizen Can Inspect a Public Officer's Asset Declaration. The National Assembly Has Spent 27 Years Not Saying How.

On Tuesday a group protested at the EFCC with a list of properties it says belong to Rabiu Kwankwaso, Peter Obi's running mate, and asked the commission to check them against his asset declarations. Nobody outside the Code of Conduct Bureau can make that comparison, for him or for any other candidate, because the terms the Constitution left the National Assembly to write in 1999 have never been written. In March 2024 the Court of Appeal confirmed that the Freedom of Information Act cannot open the files without them.

By Obongodu Paul Unanam. Published Thursday 26 November 2026. Eight minutes. On asset declarations.

On Tuesday a group called the Society for Anti Corruption and Good Governance Initiative protested at the EFCC's headquarters in Abuja and handed in a petition against Rabiu Kwankwaso, the former Kano governor who is now Peter Obi's running mate on the Nigeria Democratic Congress ticket. It names properties in Abuja, Kano and Nasarawa, among them two schools, a former hotel and two plazas, which the group says it identified through public reports. It wants a lifestyle audit, a forensic look at his bank accounts and tax records, and the findings made public before the 2027 elections.

The EFCC's chief of staff, Michael Nzekwe, received it. "EFCC is apolitical. We are not partisan." He said the commission would look into it on its merits and send it to its leadership. BusinessDay, which reported the visit, carried no response from Kwankwaso or his aides.

I am not going to tell you whether any of those properties are his, or whether he declared them. I do not know. The group does not seem to know either, since its list rests on public reports. And under the rules as they stand, you have no lawful way to check.

One line in the petition is the reason I am writing about this at all. It asks the EFCC to review his asset declarations with the Code of Conduct Bureau, from 1999 to date.

Every public officer in Nigeria, from a local government councillor to the President, has to declare everything they own and owe, and what their unmarried children under 18 own, when they take office, every four years after that, and again when they leave. The declarations go to the Code of Conduct Bureau. Then the Constitution makes a promise. Its Third Schedule tells the bureau to keep custody of the declarations and make them available for inspection by any citizen of Nigeria, "on such terms and conditions as the National Assembly may prescribe".

Twenty seven years later, the National Assembly has not prescribed them. A former chairman of the bureau said it plainly a few years ago, while promising that declarations would soon be filed online: the Assembly had given no guidelines, so "the hands of the CCB are tied". The bureau has refused requests under the Freedom of Information Act on exactly that ground. When the ICIR reported his complaint, it noted that he did not say why the bureau had not asked the Assembly to prescribe the terms.

In March 2024 the Court of Appeal agreed with the bureau. The case began in January 2017, when the African Centre for Media and Information Literacy used the FOI Act to ask how many of the National Assembly's principal officers had complied with the declaration rules, and for copies of what they declared. It lost at the Federal High Court that November and lost again on appeal, more than seven years after it first asked. The appeal court held that the general provisions of the FOI Act "cannot be employed for such inspection" until the Assembly sets its terms.

The request asked for the declarations of the National Assembly's own principal officers. The answer was that only the National Assembly can open them.

Meanwhile the bureau has been busy. In June it said it had finished verifying the declarations of 19 ministers, 37 permanent secretaries and other officials it treats as high risk, and that its online system, built to hold the declarations of every public servant in the country, was ready for deployment. The bureau is getting better at reading the forms. The public is no closer to reading them.

So when a question about a candidate's wealth comes up in an election season, the only place it can go is a petition. Someone assembles a list of properties from public reports and carries it to the EFCC, and the commission promises to be professional. If it investigates, it does so in private. Whatever it concludes will reach the rest of us as a statement, without the declarations it compared, about a running mate in the middle of a campaign, from an agency that has had to deny publicly that it targets opposition figures. Nobody is well served by that, and the voter least of all.

Look at the last thing the petition asks for: that the findings be made public before the elections. The petitioners want publication too. They are asking the EFCC for the kind of openness the Constitution promised every citizen in 1999, and they are asking an office that does not hold the declarations.

The EFCC should be the last stop for a voter's question about a candidate's wealth. In Nigeria it is the only one.

We have seen where this road goes. In January 2019 a self described civil society group called ARDI petitioned the Code of Conduct Bureau about the Chief Justice, Walter Onnoghen. The petition was dated January 7, and a charge was filed four days later. He was suspended on January 25, weeks before a general election, and in April the tribunal convicted him of failing to declare five bank accounts. In November 2024 the Court of Appeal acquitted him. The only time I can remember the public seeing inside a Chief Justice's declaration, it arrived as a charge sheet in an election season.

This is not about Kwankwaso. The same rule seals the declarations of President Tinubu, Atiku Abubakar, Peter Obi and every governor and minister in the country. In his first media chat as president, in December 2024, Tinubu said he might ask the bureau to release his own declaration. I have not seen it released. In August SERAP wrote to all 19 presidential candidates asking them to publish their declarations voluntarily, and conceded in the same letter that nothing in the Constitution requires a candidate to do so before an election. Omoyele Sowore's answer was that he had listed his assets publicly in 2022.

Nigeria has had a president who published anyway. SERAP has long pointed to Umaru Yar'Adua, who released his declarations as governor of Katsina and again as president. Nothing in the law stopped him, and nothing stops a candidate today. A declaration describes the officer's own property, and I know of no rule that prevents the officer from showing it to the public.

Other countries have answered the question the National Assembly keeps leaving open. In the Philippines, an official's sworn statement of assets and liabilities must be open for inspection at reasonable hours, can be copied ten working days after it is filed, and stays available for ten years. In India, candidates' asset declarations are public. Pakistan's Election Commission publishes its legislators' statements, and when the National Assembly there voted in January to let the Speaker withhold one where publication would pose a serious threat, it started a fight. Even that rollback is an exception carved out of a rule of publication.

The UN Convention against Corruption, which Nigeria ratified in 2004, only asks states to consider disclosure systems for public officials. Nigeria's Constitution went further than the treaty. It promised the citizen the right to look. It never said how.

The terms would not take long to write. Publish a summary of every declaration by an elected officer, a minister and the head of every agency, online, within a fixed time of filing. Leave out home addresses, account numbers and the names of children. Let any citizen inspect the full form on application, with penalties for misusing what they see. Then amend the Electoral Act so that anyone who wants to be on a ballot files a sworn statement of assets with INEC, and INEC publishes it. That is a short bill. The Assembly has had twenty seven years.

The EFCC says it will look at Tuesday's petition on its merits. Suppose it does. If Kwankwaso declared everything he owns, the secrecy is costing him, because nothing would clear him faster than his own declaration. If he did not, the secrecy is protecting him. I do not know which is true. Under the rules the National Assembly has left in place since 1999, I have no lawful way to find out, and neither do you.
